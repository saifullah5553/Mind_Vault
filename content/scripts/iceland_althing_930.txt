TITLE: How Iceland Ruled Itself Without a King: The Althing of 930
DESCRIPTION: In the year 930, the settlers of Iceland met in a rift valley at Thingvellir and founded the Althing, an assembly that made the law for a country with no king. This is the history of Iceland, from the first settlers to the republic proclaimed in the same valley in 1944.
THUMBNAIL: NO KING. ONE LAW.

[INTRODUCTION]
In the year 930, the people of Iceland rode to a crack in the earth. There, in a rift valley called Thingvellir, with no king to command them, they founded an assembly to make the law for a whole island. They called it the Althing.

Its walls were cliffs of dark rock. Its roof was the open sky. Today it is often called one of the oldest parliaments in the world.

By the end of this story, you will know how a young nation of settlers ran a country without a king, how one man under a fur cloak kept it from tearing apart, and why, a thousand years later, Iceland came back to the same valley to declare itself a republic.

[CHAPTER: Smoke Over the Bay]
The story begins with a settler named Ingólfur Arnarson. According to the Book of Settlements, written down centuries later, he arrived in Iceland in the year 874.

The old story says he threw the carved pillars of his high seat into the sea, and promised to live wherever they washed ashore. His men found them in a bay where steam rose from hot springs. He called the place Reykjavík. Smoke Bay.

Others followed fast. Most of the families came from Norway. Many others came from Ireland and Scotland. Within about sixty years, says the Book of Settlements, all the land that could be farmed had been claimed.

And that created a problem. An island full of proud, armed farmers, with no king to settle their quarrels. Who would make the law? And who would keep the peace?

[CHAPTER: The Assembly Fields]
The answer was the Althing. Every summer, chieftains and farmers travelled across the island to Thingvellir, which means the assembly fields. They camped in booths of turf and stone, and for about two weeks the empty valley became the busiest place in Iceland.

People came to trade, to arrange marriages, to hear the news, and above all to settle disputes.

At the heart of it all stood the Law Rock. From there, an elected lawspeaker recited the laws out loud. He served for three years. Each summer he spoke one third of the law, so that during his term, the whole code was heard.

In the early days, the law was not kept in a book. It lived in the lawspeaker's memory, and in the ears of the people standing below him.

Here is the strange part. The Althing made laws and judged cases, but it had no army and no police. If you won your case, it was up to you and your family to enforce the verdict. No one else would do it for you.

It sounds fragile. A country held together by nothing but agreement. And yet this commonwealth without a king lasted for more than three hundred years.

[CHAPTER: The Man Under the Cloak]
Its hardest test came around the year 1000.

By then, Christianity had reached Iceland, and the people were split. Some had taken up the new faith. Others kept the old Norse gods. At the Althing, the two sides came close to breaking apart and living under separate laws. On an island that small, two laws could easily mean war.

So they turned to the lawspeaker, a chieftain named Thorgeir. He was not a Christian. He still followed the old gods.

Thorgeir went to his booth, lay down, and pulled a fur cloak over his head. He stayed there, silent, for a day and a night.

Then he walked to the Law Rock and gave his answer. He warned that if the law was divided, the peace would be divided too. There had to be one law for everyone.

Iceland would become Christian. But people were allowed to keep some of the old customs, and to worship the old gods in private.

Think about that. A chieftain of the old faith chose the new one for the whole island, because keeping the country in one piece mattered more to him. And the Althing accepted his decision.

[CHAPTER: The End of the Commonwealth]
But the peace could not last forever. In the thirteenth century, a few powerful families fought one another for control of Iceland, in years of feuds and burning farms.

Between 1262 and 1264, the chieftains of Iceland accepted the rule of the king of Norway, in an agreement remembered as the Old Covenant. The commonwealth without a king was over.

The Althing kept meeting at Thingvellir, but now it served a king across the sea. Later, Iceland passed with Norway under the Danish crown, and power drifted further away.

The last assembly met in the valley in 1798. In 1800, a royal order abolished the Althing altogether. After nearly nine hundred years, the Law Rock fell silent.

[CHAPTER: Return to the Valley]
It did not stay silent. In the nineteenth century, Icelanders began to call for their old assembly back. In 1845, a restored Althing met for the first time. Not in the valley, as many had hoped, but in Reykjavík, the town that had grown up around Ingólfur's Smoke Bay.

Step by step, Iceland won back control of its own affairs. In 1918, it became a separate kingdom, sharing a king with Denmark.

Then, in May 1944, Icelanders voted in a referendum. Almost all of them chose to end the union and to become a republic.

On the seventeenth of June 1944, they gathered at the place where it all began. At Thingvellir, the Republic of Iceland was proclaimed, and Sveinn Björnsson became its first president. To this day, Iceland celebrates the seventeenth of June as its National Day.

[CONCLUSION]
In 1930, on the Althing's thousandth anniversary, the valley became a national park, protected for the whole nation. In 2004, it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

And geologists will tell you something remarkable about it. Thingvellir lies on the boundary between two of the great plates of the Earth's crust, the North American plate and the Eurasian plate. They are slowly pulling apart, a little further every single year. You can walk through the rift between them.

So the assembly that held a nation together met in a valley that is being torn in two.

The settlers of Iceland had no king, no army and no police. What they had was one idea: that people who disagree can still agree to live under one law.

The ground beneath Thingvellir is splitting. The idea born there is more than a thousand years old, and Iceland still lives by it.
