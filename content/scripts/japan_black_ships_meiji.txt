TITLE: Meiji Restoration: How Japan Remade Itself After the Black Ships
DESCRIPTION: In July 1853, four American warships sailed into Edo Bay and forced Japan, closed to most of the world for more than two centuries, to open its doors. This is the history of Japan's Meiji Restoration: how a land of shoguns and samurai built railways, factories, a constitution and a parliament in less than forty years.

[INTRODUCTION]
On the eighth of July 1853, four American warships sailed into Edo Bay and dropped anchor off Uraga, not far from the city we now call Tokyo. Two of them were steam warships. The Japanese called them the black ships.

Their commander, Commodore Matthew Perry, carried a letter from President Millard Fillmore. It asked Japan to open its doors.

Less than forty years later, the same country had steam trains, modern factories, a written constitution and an elected assembly. How did a nation that had kept the world at arm's length for more than two centuries remake itself so fast? And who paid the price?

[CHAPTER: The Closed Country]
For more than two hundred years, Japan's rulers had kept strict limits on contact with the outside world. The policy, later called sakoku, took shape in the 1630s.

Real power lay with the shogun, a military ruler based in Edo. The imperial court sat far away in Kyoto.

Only one European nation was allowed to trade. The Dutch. Their merchants lived on Dejima, a small artificial island off Nagasaki. Chinese traders also came and went through the port of Nagasaki. Everyone else was kept out.

So when Perry's ships appeared in the bay, they brought a question Japan could not wave away. Perry handed over the letter, promised to come back for an answer, and sailed off.

[CHAPTER: The Door Opens]
He came back in February 1854, this time with eight warships. On the thirty-first of March, at Kanagawa, near the village of Yokohama, the two sides signed a treaty. The Convention of Kanagawa opened two ports, Shimoda and Hakodate, to American ships.

It was only the start. In 1858, the American consul Townsend Harris won a far bigger deal. The Harris Treaty opened Kanagawa and four other ports to trade and fixed low duties on goods. It also said that Americans accused of crimes in Japan would be tried by their own consuls, not by Japanese courts.

Britain, France, Russia and the Netherlands soon won similar terms. History remembers them as the unequal treaties.

To many Japanese, this was a humiliation. Anger at the foreigners grew, and the shogun's government took the blame.

[CHAPTER: The Restoration]
In November 1867, the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, offered to give up his power. On the third of January 1868, the rule of the emperor was restored.

The emperor was a boy of fifteen named Mutsuhito. His reign took the name Meiji, which means enlightened rule.

Not everyone accepted the change. From January 1868 to June 1869, forces loyal to the shogun fought the emperor's supporters, in what became known as the Boshin War. The emperor's side won. Edo was renamed Tokyo, the eastern capital, and the emperor moved there.

Then the men around the young emperor did something remarkable. In 1871, they abolished the old domains, where feudal lords had ruled for centuries, and replaced them with more than three hundred prefectures. Within a year, those were merged into seventy-two, all answering to one government in Tokyo.

[CHAPTER: Seeing the World]
Next, they decided to see the West for themselves.

In December 1871, the Iwakura Mission sailed from Yokohama to San Francisco. Forty-eight officials went, with fifty-three students and attendants. They were led by Iwakura Tomomi. With him were three men who would shape modern Japan: Okubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, and a young Ito Hirobumi. Okubo came from Satsuma. Kido and Ito came from Choshu. In 1866, those two southwestern domains had joined forces against the shogun.

They toured the United States, then Britain, France, Germany and much of Europe. They studied factories, schools, armies and systems of government.

They also hoped to rewrite the old treaties. In that, they failed. The foreign powers would not agree.

But they came home in September 1873 with something more useful: a plan.

Five young girls had sailed with them, sent to be educated in America. The youngest, Tsuda Umeko, was just six years old. Years later, back in Japan, she founded a school for women in 1900. Today it is Tsuda University.

[CHAPTER: Building a New Japan]
Change did not wait for the mission's return.

In 1872, Japan's first railway opened, between Shimbashi in Tokyo and Yokohama. It was built with British money and British engineers, led by Edmund Morel.

That same year, a state silk mill opened at Tomioka. It had three hundred silk reeling machines brought from France, and a French engineer, Paul Brunat, to set it up. Silk was already Japan's biggest export, and the mill was built to make it the modern way. Today, the Tomioka Silk Mill is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

[CHAPTER: The Price]
But a new Japan left little room for the old one.

The samurai had been the warrior class of the old order. In 1873, Japan brought in conscription for all. Farmers' sons would now be called up as soldiers too.

In 1876, an edict banned the carrying of swords in public, except by soldiers and police. For the samurai, the sword had been the mark of their class. Their hereditary payments from the state were taken away as well.

Some samurai rose in revolt. The last and most serious rising came in 1877, and the government's conscript army defeated it.

The old warrior class had helped bring the emperor back. Now it had lost almost everything that made it special.

[CHAPTER: The Constitution]
On the eleventh of February 1889, the emperor gave Japan a written constitution. It was modelled mainly on Germany's, with some British ideas. The man who oversaw the drafting was Ito Hirobumi, the young official from the Iwakura Mission, who by then had become Japan's first prime minister.

In 1890, the first Imperial Diet met, with an elected lower house.

And the treaties that had shamed the country? In 1894, Britain signed a new treaty with Japan. By 1899, foreigners in Japan answered to Japanese courts.

[CONCLUSION]
In 1853, Japan watched four foreign warships sail into its bay and could not send them away. Forty-six years later, it judged foreigners under its own law.

Emperor Meiji died in 1912. In the years of his reign, a closed country of shoguns and lords became a modern state.

How did it happen so fast? Its leaders did not simply copy the West. They went to see it, chose what they wanted, and built it at home.

The black ships forced the door open. What Japan built on the other side was its own work.
