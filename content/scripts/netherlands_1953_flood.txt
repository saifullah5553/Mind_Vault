TITLE: How the Netherlands Beat the Sea After the Great Flood of 1953
DESCRIPTION: On the night of 31 January 1953, the North Sea broke through the dikes of the Netherlands and 1,836 people died. This is the story of how a country that lies partly below sea level answered with the Delta Works, some of the largest flood defences ever built.
THUMBNAIL: 1953: THE SEA RETURNED

[INTRODUCTION]
On the night of the thirty-first of January, 1953, a storm pushed the North Sea against the coast of the Netherlands. By morning, the dikes had broken in many places, and the water was inside the houses.

More than eighteen hundred people died before help could reach them. Most of them lived in the low islands of Zeeland, in the south-west of the country.

For a nation that had fought the sea for a thousand years, it was a terrible defeat. But what the Dutch did next changed how the whole world thinks about water. By the end of this story, you will know why a country that lies partly below the sea still stands, and why it may be the safest delta on Earth.

[CHAPTER: A Country Made by Hand]
To understand that night, start with the map. About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level. More than half of the country could flood, from the sea or from its great rivers.

That land was not simply given to the Dutch. Much of it was made.

For centuries, farmers built earth walls called dikes to keep the water out. Inside the dikes, they pumped the land dry. The pumps were windmills.

In the early sixteen hundreds, a lake north of Amsterdam called the Beemster was drained and turned into farmland. It became a model of Dutch land reclamation, a lake bed laid out in neat fields and straight roads. Today it is a world heritage site.

Further south, at Kinderdijk, a row of windmills was built between 1738 and 1740 to keep the land dry. They still stand along the water. UNESCO listed them in 1997.

So the windmills on postcards are not decoration. They are machines from a war that never ended. A war against water.

[CHAPTER: The First Great Wall]
In 1916, a storm flood hit the coast around the Zuiderzee, a shallow inland sea in the north. It pushed the government to act on a plan made years earlier by an engineer named Cornelis Lely.

The plan was bold. Close off the whole sea with a dam.

The dam was finished in 1932. It is called the Afsluitdijk, and it runs about thirty kilometres across open water. Behind it, the salt sea slowly turned into a freshwater lake, the IJsselmeer. New land was drained from it, land where towns and farms now stand.

It looked as if the Dutch were winning. In the north, perhaps they were. But in the south-west, where three great rivers reach the sea, the old dikes were still waiting for their test.

[CHAPTER: The Night]
The test came at the end of January 1953. A deep storm moved down the North Sea. And the tide was high, close to a spring tide.

Wind and tide combined to drive the sea higher than anyone living had seen. Along the coast of Zeeland and South Holland, the water rose over the dikes and then broke through them.

It happened on a Saturday night. Many people were asleep. In many villages, no warning reached them. They woke to the sound of water in the street, cold and black, rising fast.

Families fled into attics and onto roofs. Some waited there for days.

In the Netherlands, 1,836 people died. Tens of thousands more had to leave their homes. Across the sea, England and Belgium were flooded too, and hundreds more lost their lives there.

Now the Dutch faced a choice that would shape the next forty years. The Dutch could rebuild the old dikes. But was rebuilding enough?

[CHAPTER: Closing the Last Gap]
First, the breaches had to be closed. Soldiers, farmers and volunteers worked for months, filling the gaps with sand, clay and stone.

The last and hardest gap was at the village of Ouwerkerk. The current there was too strong for ordinary methods. So the engineers used something unusual: giant hollow concrete boxes, called caissons, built in England during the Second World War as floating harbour sections.

They floated them into place and sank them in the gap. On the night of the sixth of November, 1953, the last breach was closed.

Those same concrete boxes still stand at Ouwerkerk today. Inside them is the national museum of the flood.

[CHAPTER: The Delta Plan]
Only weeks after the disaster, the government set up a group of experts, the Delta Committee. Their answer was not to patch the old dikes. It was to shorten the coastline itself.

Instead of defending hundreds of kilometres of twisting shore, they would close the sea inlets of the south-west with dams and barriers. This became the Delta Works.

Over the next decades, dam after dam went up across the estuaries of Zeeland and South Holland. Islands that had been cut off for centuries were joined to the mainland by roads running along the tops of the new dams. A coastline that had been the country's weakest point became its strongest.

Then came a twist. The last great inlet, the Eastern Scheldt, was rich in fish, shellfish and birds. A solid dam would have turned it into a dead lake. People protested, and the plan changed.

Instead of a wall, the engineers built a barrier with huge steel gates that normally stay open. The tide flows in and out. Only when a dangerous storm comes do the gates come down. The Eastern Scheldt barrier opened in October 1986.

Was that the end? Not quite.

[CHAPTER: The Gates of Rotterdam]
Rotterdam is one of the busiest ports in the world. Its waterway to the sea could not be closed by a dam, because ships pass through it day and night.

So in 1997, the Dutch finished the Maeslant barrier. Two enormous gates rest in dry docks on either bank. When the water near Rotterdam is forecast to rise more than three metres above average sea level, a computer gives the order. The gates float out, fill with water, and sink to close the river.

No person has to push the button. The decision is made by the system itself.

[CONCLUSION]
In 1953, the sea came at night, and the country was not ready. Today, the Dutch measure flood risk in chances of one in many thousands of years, and engineers from around the world come to learn from their dams, gates and dikes.

But the lesson they took from 1953 was not that the sea can be beaten. It was that the sea never stops testing you. So you never stop building.

The water that took everything in 1953 is still out there. It is just on the other side of the wall.
