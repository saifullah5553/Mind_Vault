TITLE: Norway 1905: How a Nation Left Sweden Without a War and Chose a King

[INTRODUCTION]
On the twenty-fifth of November, 1905, a Danish prince stepped ashore in Kristiania, today's Oslo, with his two-year-old son in his arms.
A week earlier, he had been Prince Carl of Denmark.
Now he was Haakon the Seventh, King of Norway, and the Norwegian people had just voted to have a king at all.
Hello, friends, and welcome to your own channel, Mind Vault. Today's story: how Norway broke free and chose a king.
In a single summer, Norway left its union with Sweden. Armies faced each other across the border, and no shot was fired.
By the end of this story, you will know how a parliament removed a king without fighting, and why a free country then voted for a new one.

[CHAPTER: Eidsvoll]
The story begins in 1814, at the end of the Napoleonic Wars.
In the Treaty of Kiel, the king of Denmark and Norway gave Norway away, to the king of Sweden.
Norway was not asked.
So the Norwegians refused.
One hundred and twelve elected men gathered at Eidsvoll, a country estate north of Kristiania.
On the seventeenth of May, 1814, they signed a constitution of their own, and Norway still celebrates that day every year.
Sweden's answer came in late July. Swedish troops marched into Norway, and the resistance did not last long.
A ceasefire, the Convention of Moss, followed on the fourteenth of August.
But here is the surprise. Sweden accepted Norway's new constitution.
Norway would keep its own parliament, the Storting, its own courts, its own army, its own flag and its own money.
It would share only two things with Sweden: a king, and a foreign policy run from Stockholm.

[CHAPTER: The Consuls]
For ninety years, that strange marriage held.
But a country with its own parliament wanted its own voice abroad.
The quarrel came down to consuls, the officials who look after a nation's ships and traders in foreign ports.
Norway wanted its own.
In March 1905, a shipowner from Bergen, Christian Michelsen, became prime minister.
His government had one stated goal: a separate Norwegian consular service.
The Storting passed the law in May.
On the twenty-seventh of May, 1905, in Stockholm, King Oscar the Second refused to sign it.
His Norwegian ministers would not put their names to his refusal. They resigned, all of them.
And the king would not accept their resignations. He could not find anyone to replace them.
It looked like a dead end. It was a door.

[CHAPTER: The Seventh of June]
The ministers travelled from Stockholm to Kristiania.
On the seventh of June, 1905, the Storting met.
It passed a resolution, without a single vote against.
The king, it said, had been unable to give Norway a government. And so, in its words, "the constitutional monarchy has ceased to exist."
The union with Sweden was dissolved.
The resigned ministers were told to carry on, and to use the king's powers themselves.
Think about what had just happened. Norway had not overthrown its king. It had simply announced that he had stopped being its king.
In Sweden, many were furious.
Sweden insisted that the Norwegian people should vote on it.
Norway had already chosen a date: the thirteenth of August.
Only men could vote in Norway then. Women gathered more than two hundred thousand signatures of their own, in support.
The result was almost unbelievable.
Three hundred and sixty-eight thousand, two hundred and eight men voted to end the union.
One hundred and eighty-four voted no.

[CHAPTER: Karlstad]
But a vote is not a peace treaty.
That summer, soldiers were quietly sent to both sides of the border.
In places, the two armies stood only two kilometres apart.
Many in Sweden did not want war. The Swedish Social Democrats spoke out against calling up the reserves.
On the thirty-first of August, delegates from both countries met at Karlstad, a town in western Sweden.
For more than three weeks, they argued.
On the twenty-third of September, they agreed.
There would be a neutral zone along the border, and Norwegian forts inside it were pulled down. At Ørje, the guns were taken away.
The Storting accepted the terms on the ninth of October. Sweden's parliament followed four days later.
On the twenty-sixth of October, 1905, King Oscar the Second gave up the throne of Norway, and Sweden recognised Norway as an independent country.
Ninety-one years of union were over, without a single battle.

[CHAPTER: A King by Vote]
Now Norway had a new question. Who would lead it?
The answer was a young Danish prince, Carl.
He was the second son of Crown Prince Frederik of Denmark.
His wife, Maud, was the youngest daughter of Britain's King Edward the Seventh.
They had a little son, Alexander, born in 1903.
Some Norwegians wanted a republic instead. So Carl set a condition.
He refused to take the crown unless the people voted for a monarchy first, and parliament then chose him.
So Norway voted again, on the twelfth and thirteenth of November.
Two hundred and fifty-nine thousand, five hundred and sixty-three voted for a king.
Sixty-nine thousand, two hundred and sixty-four voted for a republic.
About seventy-nine percent, for the crown.
On the eighteenth of November, the Storting elected Carl as King of Norway.
He accepted that same evening and took the name Haakon.
His son Alexander became Olav, Crown Prince of Norway.
A week later, the new king came ashore at Vippetangen, in Kristiania, carrying Olav on his arm.
Two days after that, he swore his oath to the constitution, the one first written at Eidsvoll in 1814.
On the twenty-second of June, 1906, Haakon and Maud were crowned in Nidaros Cathedral in Trondheim.

[CONCLUSION]
Thirty-five years later, that vote was put to the test.
In April 1940, Nazi Germany invaded Norway.
The Germans demanded that the king accept a puppet government led by Vidkun Quisling.
Haakon refused. He said he would give up the throne rather than accept it.
He went into exile in Great Britain, and Norway's government carried on from London.
In June 1945, after the defeat of Germany, he came home.
He reigned until his death on the twenty-first of September, 1957.
Norway did not win its freedom on a battlefield. It won it with a resolution, two votes, and a treaty signed at Karlstad.
And the king the people chose kept faith with them when it mattered most.
