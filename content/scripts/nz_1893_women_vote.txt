[INTRODUCTION]
On the twenty-eighth of July, 1893, a roll of paper more than two hundred and seventy metres long was carried into New Zealand's Parliament.
When it was unwound, it stretched the whole length of the debating chamber.
It was a petition, signed by more than twenty-five thousand women.

At that time, no self-governing country in the world let women vote for its parliament.
Seven weeks later, New Zealand did.
By the end of this story, you will know how a petition, a white flower, and a premier's trick that went wrong changed the world.

[CHAPTER: Kate Sheppard]
The woman behind the roll was Kate Sheppard.
She was born in Liverpool, in England.
In 1868, her family sailed from England to New Zealand, and she made her home in Christchurch.
There, she joined the Women's Christian Temperance Union.
The union campaigned against alcohol.
To change the drinking laws, its members needed votes. And women had none.
In 1887, the union put Kate Sheppard in charge of its campaign for the vote.
She wrote letters to newspapers, and in 1888 a pamphlet called Ten Reasons Why the Women of New Zealand Should Vote.
She spoke at meetings, and she wrote to members of Parliament, again and again.

[CHAPTER: The Petitions]
The campaign's weapon was the petition, a long list of names asking Parliament to act.
In 1891, more than nine thousand women signed one.
The lower house passed a bill to give women the vote.
The upper house, the Legislative Council, threw it out.
In 1892, they tried again. This time, almost twenty thousand signed.
Again, the bill died in Parliament.
Two defeats. Many campaigns would have stopped there.
This one went bigger.
Sheets of paper were sent around the country, to towns, villages and farms.
Women aged twenty-one and over signed their names, one line at a time.
The sheets came back to Christchurch.
Kate Sheppard pasted them together, end to end, and rolled them around a piece of broom handle.
More than five hundred sheets became one enormous roll.
It was meant for women, but at least twenty men signed it too.
Not everyone was pleased.
The liquor trade feared that women would vote to close the bars.
Henry Fish, a member of Parliament for South Dunedin, organised petitions against women voting.
His side gathered about five thousand signatures.
Kate Sheppard's side gathered nearly thirty-two thousand.
That was almost one in four of the adult European women in the colony.
Thirteen petitions went to Parliament that year. Twelve of them were small. One was a giant.

[CHAPTER: The Trick]
On the twenty-eighth of July, 1893, Sir John Hall presented the great roll to Parliament.
Staff unwound part of it, all the way down the chamber.
At its head was one name: Mary J. Carpenter, and twenty-five thousand, five hundred and nineteen others.
The lower house passed the new Electoral Bill.
But the bill still had to pass the Legislative Council, the upper house.
Its members were not elected. They were appointed, and it had killed the bill twice before.
And there, it had a powerful enemy.
In April 1893, the premier, John Ballance, had died. Ballance had backed the women's cause.
The new premier, Richard Seddon, did not want women to vote.
Many of them supported banning alcohol, and Seddon did not.
He counted the votes in the Council. He was just one vote short of stopping the bill.
One councillor, Thomas Kelly, had promised to support the bill.
Seddon sent him a telegram, and won his consent to change his vote.
It looked as if the bill was dead. Seddon thought he had won.
Then the trick backfired.
Two councillors, William Reynolds and Edward Stevens, had opposed the bill.
They were so angry at Seddon's manipulation that they changed sides.
The supporters had given their friends in Parliament white camellias to wear in their buttonholes.
On the eighth of September, 1893, the Council voted.
Twenty for, eighteen against.
The bill had passed.
Even then, the fight was not quite over.
Eighteen councillors asked the governor, Lord Glasgow, to refuse to sign it.
He did not refuse.
On the nineteenth of September, 1893, the governor signed the Electoral Act.
New Zealand had become the first self-governing country in the world where women could vote in parliamentary elections.

[CHAPTER: The First Vote]
The general election was set for the twenty-eighth of November.
That left women about ten weeks to get their names on the roll.
They did it, in their thousands.
On election day, women walked to the polling booths for the first time.
Māori women voted too, in the Māori electorates, on the twentieth of December.
About sixty-five percent of the women who could vote did so.
For the first time, the women of a whole country had a say in who governed them.
That same year, Elizabeth Yates was elected mayor of Onehunga, near Auckland.
She won by just thirteen votes.
She was the first woman in the British Empire to be elected mayor.
The rest of the world took its time.
In Britain, some women won the vote in 1918, and only if they were over thirty.
In the United States, women won it across the country in 1920.

[CONCLUSION]
The victory had a limit.
Women could vote, but they could not stand for Parliament until 1919.
The first woman member of Parliament, Elizabeth McCombs, was elected in 1933, forty years after women first voted.
Kate Sheppard kept working. In 1896, she became the first president of the National Council of Women of New Zealand.
She died in Christchurch in 1934.
In 1991, her portrait was placed on New Zealand's ten-dollar note.
And the roll she pasted together still survives.
Twelve of the smaller petitions were lost. The great one was not.
Today it is kept in Wellington, and UNESCO lists it as part of the Memory of the World.
And every year, on the nineteenth of September, New Zealand marks Suffrage Day.
Two hundred and seventy metres of paper. Twenty-five thousand names, written one at a time.
And a premier whose own trick turned against him.
