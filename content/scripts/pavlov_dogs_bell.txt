TITLE: Pavlov's Dogs: The Bell Myth and the Real Discovery
DESCRIPTION: Ivan Pavlov's dogs are famous for drooling at the sound of a bell, but his biographer says Pavlov didn't use one for his real experiments. This is the true story of Pavlov and his dogs: how a priest's son from Ryazan won a Nobel Prize for digestion, discovered the conditioned reflex, and set out to study the mind.
THUMBNAIL: THERE WAS NO BELL
TAGS: pavlov's dogs, ivan pavlov, classical conditioning, conditioned reflex, pavlov bell myth, psychic secretion, history of psychology, behaviourism, john b watson, daniel todes, nobel prize 1904, pavlov's dog monument, psychology documentary

[INTRODUCTION]
Everyone knows the story of Ivan Pavlov's dogs. A bell rings, and the dogs begin to drool. It is one of the most famous experiments in the history of psychology.

There is just one problem. According to his biographer, Daniel Todes, Pavlov didn't use a bell. And for his real scientific work, he couldn't.

By the end of this story, you will know who Pavlov really was, what his dogs actually taught us, and why the man who gave us the conditioned reflex believed he was studying something much bigger than drool. He was studying the mind.

So how did a story about drool become one of the best-known ideas in psychology? And how did it get the most famous detail wrong?

[CHAPTER: The Priest's Son]
Ivan Pavlov was born on the twenty-sixth of September, 1849, in Ryazan, a city south-east of Moscow. His father was a village priest of the Russian Orthodox Church.

Pavlov was expected to follow him. He studied at a seminary, the local school for priests. But he left without graduating.

In 1870, he travelled from Ryazan to Saint Petersburg, then the capital of the Russian Empire, and enrolled at the university to study natural science. He would become a physiologist, a scientist of how the body works.

Success came slowly. He got his first real job, Todes notes, at the age of forty-one.

[CHAPTER: The Digestion Machine]
In 1891, Pavlov was invited to the Institute of Experimental Medicine in Saint Petersburg, to organise and direct its department of physiology.

His great subject was digestion. How does the body turn a meal into fuel? And what tells the stomach when to start? Using surgery, his team redirected the dogs' digestive juices to the outside of the body, where they could be collected and measured.

It worked. In 1904, Pavlov won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, in recognition of his work on the physiology of digestion. He was fifty-five years old, and at last he was famous.

Here is the first surprise. The Nobel Prize was not for the dogs and the bell. It was not for conditioning at all. It was for the stomach.

[CHAPTER: The Wrong Kind of Drool]
But the dogs did something unexpected. They began to salivate before the food arrived. They drooled in the presence of the technician who normally fed them.

Pavlov called it psychic secretion. And instead of ignoring it, he set out to study it.

The idea was simple. Food in the mouth makes a dog salivate. No learning is needed. That is an unconditioned reflex, and the food is the unconditioned stimulus. But if another signal comes again and again just before the food, the dog learns to link the two. Soon the signal alone brings the drool. The signal has become a conditioned stimulus. Nothing about the sound itself is tasty. The dog has learned what it means.

Pavlov's question was never about dogs alone. It was about how any animal, including us, learns to expect what comes next.

In the classic example, that signal was the sound of a metronome. Pavlov called the learned response a conditional reflex. In English, it became the conditioned reflex.

And it can be undone. Sound the signal again and again with no food, and the drool fades away. Psychologists call this extinction.

Today, this kind of learning is called classical conditioning, or Pavlovian conditioning, after the man who studied it in his dogs.

[CHAPTER: The Bell That Never Rang]
Ask most people about Pavlov, and the bell is the one detail they know. It is also the detail that is wrong.

So where did the bell come from?

Todes says a bell could not be controlled precisely enough. Pavlov needed signals he could control exactly. His laboratory used metronomes, buzzers, whistles, tuning forks, visual signals, and even electric shocks.

The bell, Todes suggests, comes from a translation. The Russian word zvonok means a buzzer. In English, it became a bell. And the bell stuck.

[CHAPTER: Dogs With Personalities]
The bigger myth is about Pavlov himself. He is often pictured as a cold scientist who cared only about reflexes and ignored the mind.

Todes argues the opposite. In Pavlov's own words, "Essentially, only one thing in life is of real interest to us." For Pavlov, that one thing was our inner, psychical experience.

He saw the conditional reflex as a tool for exploring the animal and human psyche. He believed his dogs had their own personalities, emotions and thoughts. His laboratory dogs were even given names that matched their characters.

He even studied what he saw as nervous breakdowns in dogs. Some of his best-known cases followed a severe flood in 1924, in Saint Petersburg, which by then had been renamed Leningrad. Others came from giving dogs especially difficult conditioning tasks. A mind, even a dog's mind, could be pushed too far.

[CHAPTER: A Critic in Leningrad]
Then came revolution. After the Bolsheviks seized power in 1917, Pavlov seriously considered leaving Russia.

He stayed. He became one of very few public critics of the Bolsheviks' political repression. He protested when the new state repressed religion, and he gave money to the local church near his laboratory at Koltushi, outside the city. Yet he also praised the state for its great support of science. Lenin himself praised him. And Pavlov kept working to the end of his life.

[CHAPTER: The Legacy]
Far away, in America, his ideas spread. Classical conditioning became the foundation of behaviourism, a school of psychology spread by the writings of the American psychologist John Watson, and later B. F. Skinner. It offered a way to explain how habits are learned, and how they can fade.

Ivan Pavlov died in Leningrad on the twenty-seventh of February, 1936. He was eighty-six, and the cause was double pneumonia.

A year before, a bronze monument to a dog by the sculptor Bezpalov was set up on the grounds of his institute, where it still stands. It carries carved scenes and texts written by Pavlov himself. It was built on Pavlov's own initiative. It honours the laboratory dogs that made his work possible.

[CONCLUSION]
So the next time you hear someone say they are drooling like Pavlov's dog, remember the real story.

The man remembered for a bell was really chasing the mind. And the bell itself was a mistake of translation.

There was probably no bell. There was a metronome, a buzzer, and a scientist who noticed the wrong kind of drool, and refused to ignore it.

The famous bell is mostly a myth. But the idea Pavlov found in his dogs still shapes how we understand learning, habits and the mind.
