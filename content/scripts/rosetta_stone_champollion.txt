TITLE: The Rosetta Stone: How One Broken Slab Made Egypt's Pharaohs Speak
DESCRIPTION: In July 1799, French soldiers in Egypt pulled a broken slab out of an old fort wall near Rashid, and found the same decree written in hieroglyphs, Demotic and Greek. This is the story of the Rosetta Stone, and how Thomas Young and Jean-François Champollion raced to read a script nobody had understood for more than a thousand years.
THUMBNAIL: THE CODE BREAKER

[INTRODUCTION]
In July 1799, French soldiers working on an old fort near Rashid, in Egypt's Nile Delta, found a broken slab of dark stone built into a wall. Its face was covered in writing. Three kinds of writing.

One of them was Greek, and scholars could read it. One was hieroglyphs, the picture signs of the pharaohs. And nobody alive could read those.

By the end of this story, you will know what that stone really says, how two brilliant men raced each other to crack it, and why a broken slab from a fort wall gave a whole civilisation back its voice.

[CHAPTER: The Fort Wall]
The stone was found by a French officer named Pierre-François Bouchard. The fort was Fort Julien, and the town was Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta. That is how the slab got its name.

The French were in Egypt because, a year earlier, Napoleon had marched into Egypt with his army. With the soldiers came a corps of about one hundred and fifty scholars: engineers, artists and scientists, sent to study the country.

They soon understood what the soldiers had found. It was the first text in ancient Egyptian and another language to be recovered in modern times. The stone carried three blocks of text. Hieroglyphs at the top. In the middle, Demotic, the everyday script of later Egypt. And at the bottom, Greek.

If the three said the same thing, the Greek could unlock the other two. Copies of the inscriptions soon began to travel to scholars across Europe.

[CHAPTER: Captured in Egypt]
But the French never took the stone home. In 1801, their army in Egypt surrendered, and under the Capitulation of Alexandria, the stone passed to the British.

A British warship carried it across the Mediterranean, and it landed at Portsmouth in February 1802. That same year it went on display at the British Museum. It has been there ever since.

Its sides were painted with two lines. Captured in Egypt by the British Army in 1801. Presented by King George the Third.

For years, visitors thought it was black basalt. In fact it is granodiorite. The dark colour came from wax and chalk that were rubbed into it to make the writing stand out. It stands about one hundred and twelve centimetres tall, and weighs around seven hundred and sixty kilograms.

[CHAPTER: What the Stone Says]
In 1803, the Greek text was translated in full. And it was a surprise. The stone was not a great poem or a secret. It was a decree.

In 196 BC, a council of priests gathered at Memphis, the old royal city of Egypt. They passed a decree in honour of the young king Ptolemy the Fifth, who had been crowned at Memphis the year before, while still a boy.

The decree ends with an order: a copy was to be placed in every temple. So stone carvers carved the same decree three times, in three scripts.

The Rosetta Stone is only a fragment of one of those copies. Most of the hieroglyphic part is missing. Only its last fourteen lines survive. The Demotic has thirty-two lines. The Greek has fifty-four.

[CHAPTER: The Forgotten Script]
Why could nobody read the hieroglyphs? Because the last known hieroglyphic inscription was carved in the year 394, at the temple of Philae. As the old temples closed, the scribes who could read and write the signs disappeared.

For more than fourteen centuries, the carved signs on Egypt's obelisks and temples were silent. And in Europe, it was widely believed that hieroglyphs stood only for ideas, never for sounds. Each sign, they thought, was a hidden symbol. That belief would have to break before anyone could read them.

[CHAPTER: The Doctor From London]
The first man to make real progress was Thomas Young, an English doctor and scientist, famous for his work on light.

He began studying Egyptian writing in 1813. By 1814, he had worked through the Demotic text on the stone. In the hieroglyphs, some signs were circled by an oval ring, called a cartouche. Young matched one of them to the name Ptolemy in the Greek. So some signs, at least, stood for sounds.

But he believed sound signs were used only for foreign names, like the Greek name Ptolemy. That belief held him back.

[CHAPTER: The Boy From Figeac]
His rival was a Frenchman. Jean-François Champollion was born in 1790, in the small town of Figeac. By his teens he had studied many languages, and he learned to read and speak Coptic, the last form of the Egyptian language, written in Greek letters.

That gave him an edge. When he sounded out the signs, he could hear Egyptian words.

In 1822, in cartouches copied from Egyptian temples, he read two names. Ramesses. And Thutmose. These were not foreign names. They were Egyptian pharaohs, spelled with sound signs.

So the whole system used sounds, mixed with signs for meaning. On the fourteenth of September 1822, the story goes, he ran to his brother and cried out, I've got it! Then he fainted.

Two weeks later, on the twenty-seventh of September, he presented his famous Letter to Monsieur Dacier at the Academy in Paris. Thomas Young was there to hear it.

[CHAPTER: The Price of Fame]
Not everyone was generous. Some accused Champollion of failing to give Young enough credit for his early discoveries. The argument went on for years.

In 1824, Champollion published a fuller account of the system. In 1828, he finally sailed from Toulon to Egypt, and read the temple walls in person.

He died in Paris in 1832. He was only forty-one.

Young's memorial in Westminster Abbey later praised him as the man who first penetrated the obscurity that had veiled the hieroglyphs of Egypt. Today, both names are tied to the stone.

[CONCLUSION]
And the stone itself? Today it is the most visited object in the British Museum.

In 1917, during the First World War, the museum moved it underground, to a station of the Postal Tube Railway near Holborn. In 1999, it was cleaned, and the dark grey rock showed a pink vein running through it.

In October 1972, for one month, the stone crossed to Paris. At the Louvre, it was shown beside Champollion's Letter, one hundred and fifty years after he wrote it.

Its name has become a phrase. A Rosetta Stone now means the essential clue to a new field of knowledge.

A council of priests wanted to honour a boy king. Two thousand years later, their broken decree gave the pharaohs their voices back.
