TITLE: Terracotta Army: The Farmers Who Dug a Well and Found an Emperor's Army
DESCRIPTION: In March 1974, farmers digging a well near Xi'an hit a clay head, and uncovered the Terracotta Army of Qin Shi Huang, China's first emperor. This is the story of the man who wanted to live forever, the 8,000 clay soldiers he took to his grave, and the tomb beside them that has still never been opened.

[INTRODUCTION]
In March 1974, a group of farmers near Xi'an began digging a well in their orchard. As they dug, a spade struck something hard. It was a head, made of clay, with a face. Hello, friends, and welcome to your own channel, Mind Vault. Today's story: how China buried an army of clay. Under that field stood an army of thousands, waiting in the dark for more than two thousand years. It was built for one man, the first emperor of China. By the end of this story you will know who he was, why he wanted an army in his grave, and why the tomb beside it has still never been opened.

[CHAPTER: The Well]
The farmers were Yang Zhifa, his five brothers and a neighbour, Wang Puzhi. Their village, Xiyang, lay in Lintong county, east of Xi'an. They were looking for water. They found pieces of pottery and a sculpted head. A kilometre and a half to the west rose a great earth mound. The find was reported, and archaeologists came. What they uncovered was a pit about seven metres deep. In it stood rank after rank of life-size clay soldiers. In that single pit, about six thousand of them.

[CHAPTER: The Boy King]
To understand why, we have to go back more than two thousand years. In 259 BC, a boy named Ying Zheng was born in the city of Handan. At thirteen, he became king of Qin, one of several states fighting for control of China. In the same year, work began on his tomb. He would spend his whole reign building it. By 221 BC, he had conquered all the other Warring States. For the first time, China was one country under one ruler. He took a new title, Shi Huangdi, the first emperor. Today we call him Qin Shi Huang. He standardized the writing system, the currency and the measurements, so that one empire could run on one set of rules.

[CHAPTER: The Elixir]
But the most powerful man in China had one enemy he could not defeat. Death. He sent ships out to the eastern sea to search for an elixir of life. The searchers never brought one back. In 210 BC, during his fifth tour of eastern China, the first emperor died at a place called Shaqiu, far from his capital. He was forty-nine years old. The man who had united China could not buy one more year. But he had already prepared another way to last: an empire beneath the earth.

[CHAPTER: The Historian's Warning]
About a century later, the great historian Sima Qian described the tomb in his Records of the Grand Historian. He was writing about a ruler who had died before he was born. He wrote that 700,000 men were sent from all over the empire to build it. He wrote that mercury was used to make the hundred rivers and the great sea. And he wrote that craftsmen made crossbows primed to shoot anyone who broke in. It sounded like legend. And here is the strange thing. Sima Qian never mentioned a clay army. For two thousand years, nobody knew it was there.

[CHAPTER: The Army]
The digging did not stop at one pit. In May 1976, drilling found a second. That July, a third. Today we know there are four pits, about a kilometre and a half east of the tomb mound. The first holds the infantry. The second holds cavalry and more foot soldiers. The third looks like a command post, with high-ranking officers and a chariot. The fourth is empty, perhaps left unfinished by its builders. Altogether, archaeologists estimate more than eight thousand soldiers, one hundred and thirty chariots with five hundred and twenty horses, and one hundred and fifty cavalry horses. The soldiers stand between about one metre seventy-five and two metres tall. The officers are usually the tallest.

[CHAPTER: Faces From Moulds]
How do you make eight thousand soldiers? In workshops, like a factory. The bodies were built from moulds, and the heads came from a small number of basic face shapes. Then sculptors added the details by hand: moustaches, hair, the set of a mouth. The result is an army where every face looks like a different man. They also carried real weapons. Some forty thousand bronze weapons have been found, among them crossbows, arrowheads and spears. For years, people said the bronze had been coated in chromium, a secret ancient rust-proofing. A 2019 study found no such thing. The traces of chromium most likely came from the lacquer nearby. The real secret was the soil. Its chemistry, and the size of its grains, kept the bronze from rusting away.

[CHAPTER: The Lost Colours]
Here is something most people never picture. The army was not grey. The soldiers were painted in bright colours over a coat of lacquer. When a painted figure is lifted into the dry air, that lacquer can curl in fifteen seconds and flake off in four minutes. The first excavators watched the colour fall away in front of them. In later digs, the moment painted clay was uncovered, workers sprayed it with a protective solution and wrapped it in plastic to keep it moist. Since 1974, one worker, Yang Rongrong, has spent her life piecing broken soldiers back together. She has rebuilt an army of about a thousand warriors.

[CHAPTER: The Tomb]
And the emperor himself? His tomb lies under a mound seventy-six metres tall, shaped like a flattened pyramid. Around it, the whole burial complex covers about thirty-five square miles. The clay army is only one corner of it. In 2005, a team tested four thousand soil samples from the mound for mercury. All came back highly positive. In another survey, one spot measured about seven times the average of the rest. Sima Qian's rivers of mercury may be more than legend. Yet the central tomb has never been excavated. Less than one percent of the whole burial complex has been dug. One reason is fear of what air would do to whatever lies inside. The army taught that lesson in four minutes. In 1987, the mausoleum and its army were added to the world heritage list.

[CONCLUSION]
The first emperor searched the sea for a way to live forever, and he failed. He built an army to guard him, and history forgot it. Then, two thousand years later, a few farmers went looking for water. They did not find water. They found the emperor's army, still standing in its ranks, still guarding a tomb that nobody has dared to open.
